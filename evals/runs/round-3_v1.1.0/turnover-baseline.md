## What the argument is really about

Your team is arguing about method: plan first, or act first. Underneath that is a problem neither side has named yet. **You don't know why nurses are leaving.** "Exit interviews give mixed reasons" means you don't have a diagnosis.

That changes how each position looks:

- **A detailed plan right now** would be built on guesses. It would look rigorous, but it could put months of effort into the wrong causes.
- **"Try things and see" right now** would also be guessing. Without a baseline or a clear idea of what each change should fix, you won't be able to tell what worked. Turnover is noisy and slow to move, so you could easily misread random variation as a result.

Each side is right about the other side's weakness. The planners are right that random action won't teach you anything. The experimenters are right that a long plan without a diagnosis will stall. You don't need to pick one. You need to do the steps in the right order: **diagnose quickly, then run structured experiments, then commit to a plan built on what works.**

## Why "mixed reasons" may not be as mixed as it looks

Exit interviews are a weak tool for finding causes:

- **People leaving soften their reasons.** "Better opportunity" or "schedule" is safer to say than "my charge nurse" or "I didn't feel safe on nights."
- **They come too late.** You hear from people who already decided to go, not from the ones who are thinking about it.
- **One overall number hides patterns.** A 28% rate for the whole unit can come from very different problems. A few groups to check:
  - **Tenure.** Is turnover concentrated among nurses in their first year? Early turnover is common in nursing and usually points to onboarding, preceptorship, or transition-to-practice problems.
  - **Shift.** Are nights or weekends losing more people?
  - **Role or FTE status.** Full-time, part-time, and PRN staff leave for different reasons.
  - **Where they go.** Another unit in your hospital, a competing hospital, agency or travel work, or out of bedside nursing altogether. Each points to a different cause.
  - **Leader.** Does turnover cluster under a particular manager or charge nurse?
  - **Timing.** Did it rise after a staffing-model change, a leadership change, a jump in patient acuity, or a shift in census?

Once you split the data this way, "mixed reasons" often turns into two or three patterns you can actually work on.

## A path both camps can accept

**Phase 1: Fast diagnosis (about 4–6 weeks, not 6 months)**
- Break down the past 2–3 years of separations using the groups above. HR and staffing data usually already contain this.
- Hold **stay interviews** with current nurses, ideally led by someone outside their reporting line. Ask what keeps them, what would make them leave, and what they would change first.
- Check the operating data: overtime hours, float and agency use, nurse-to-patient ratios by shift, missed breaks, and workplace violence incidents.
- Put a dollar figure on the problem. Turnover costs for one bedside RN are commonly estimated in the tens of thousands of dollars once you count recruiting, orientation, overtime, and agency backfill. Use your hospital's own numbers. That figure is what will earn this work real attention.

**Phase 2: Targeted experiments (about 3–6 months)**
- Choose 2–3 interventions that match what you found, not a long menu.
- For each one, write down before you start:
  - which cause it addresses
  - which group of nurses it targets
  - which **leading indicator** you'll track, since turnover itself moves too slowly
  - what result counts as "working"
- Useful leading indicators include intent-to-stay scores, how often people call out sick, overtime and agency hours, transfer requests, and the share of first-year nurses still on the unit at 90 days and 6 months.
- Examples of good matches: if first-year turnover dominates, strengthen preceptorship or residency support. If nights are losing people, look at shift differentials, self-scheduling, or night-shift leadership. If one leader stands out, coach that leader or reassign them.

**Phase 3: Commit**
- Scale what worked, drop what didn't, and write *that* into the retention plan. By then the plan rests on evidence and has a real chance of being carried out.

## Things your team may not have said out loud

1. **Is this really a disagreement about risk and accountability?** People who want a detailed plan often want something they can defend to executives. People who want to "try things" are often tired of studying problems that never get fixed. If so, the phased approach resolves it because it gives each group what it actually needs: a defensible process for one, quick visible action for the other.
2. **Is part of the cause something leadership can't easily fix?** That could be pay against the regional market, an acuity model nobody owns, or a specific manager. Teams sometimes argue about method because the real answer is uncomfortable.
3. **What turnover rate are you aiming for, and why?** Recent national surveys have put hospital RN turnover in the high teens, so 28% is clearly high. But some turnover is healthy. Decide whether the goal is "below national average," "half of what it is now," or "keep the nurses we most need to keep." That choice affects which interventions make sense.
4. **Who owns this?** A split leadership team usually means nobody does. Name one person who answers for the diagnosis and the experiments, with a date to report back.

## The short version

Frame it for your team like this: *"We don't yet know enough to write a plan, and we don't know enough to experiment well. Let's spend six weeks finding out why nurses leave, then run a few focused experiments with clear measures, then build the plan from what works."* That respects what both sides care about and replaces the either/or choice with a sequence.
