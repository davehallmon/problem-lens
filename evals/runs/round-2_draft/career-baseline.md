The real question isn't "stable job or startup." It's whether this particular startup, on these terms, at this point in your life, is a risk you can absorb if it goes badly. Startups fail often, and you have two kids and six months of savings, so the analysis should start with the downside and work up from there.

## 1. Your actual runway is shorter than six months

Six months of savings sounds reasonable until you account for:

- **Health insurance.** If the startup's benefits are thin, or there's a gap before they start, family coverage can cost $1,500 to $2,500 or more a month and eat your runway quickly.
- **Salary cut.** If the offer is below your current pay, you'll draw on savings every month even while the startup is doing fine. Six months of savings plus a $2,000 monthly shortfall means you're out of cushion in about a year without anything going wrong.
- **Job search time.** If the startup folds, finding a comparable role at your level often takes 3 to 6 months, sometimes longer in a slow market. Six months covers one failure with almost nothing left over.

**Stress test:** Assume the startup shuts down 12 months in, with no severance. Could your family get through that without serious hardship? If not, either the terms need to change or your savings need to grow first.

## 2. Evaluate the startup, not "startups"

The risk varies enormously from one company to the next. Before deciding, find out:

- **Funding and runway.** When did they last raise, how much, and how many months of cash do they have? Under 18 months means they'll need to raise again soon, and that's a real risk.
- **Traction.** Paying customers and growing revenue, or still pre-product-market-fit?
- **The founders.** Have they built companies before? Talk to people who've worked with them.
- **Your role.** Is it clearly defined, or vague? Vague roles at early-stage companies tend to get cut or rewritten.
- **The equity, honestly.** What percentage of the company does it represent, what's the strike price, what's the vesting schedule, and how long do you have to exercise if you leave? Treat equity as a lottery ticket with better odds, not as compensation. Make the decision on cash pay and assume the equity ends up worth zero.

A Series B company with revenue, a solid team, and two years of runway is a very different decision from a seed-stage company with eight people.

## 3. Be clear about what you're trying to get

What's pulling you toward this? Your answer changes the analysis:

- **Faster growth or broader scope?** An 8-year professional can often get that through a stretch role or an internal move, with less risk.
- **Interest in the mission or product?** That's a legitimate reason, but check whether it would survive a hard year.
- **Escaping your current job?** Then the startup may be a solution to the wrong problem. Other stable employers exist.
- **Financial upside?** Look hard at the equity math. For most employees, the expected value is modest.
- **Wanting to build something before it's "too late"?** Worth taking seriously. At 8 years in, you have enough experience to be valuable to a startup and enough career left to recover if it doesn't work.

## 4. What's in your favor

- **Eight years of experience is real insurance.** You're employable. If the startup fails, you'll most likely land somewhere; the question is just how long that takes and how hard it is on your family in between.
- **Startup experience often helps your career,** even when the company fails, because you get broader ownership and visible impact.
- **Staying has risks too.** "Stable" jobs get cut in layoffs as well, and years of stagnation have a real, if less visible, cost.

## 5. Lower the risk before you jump

You don't have to take the offer as it stands:

- **Negotiate cash over equity.** At your stage of life, a higher base salary is worth more than extra options.
- **Confirm benefits in writing,** especially health coverage and when it starts.
- **Build savings to 9 to 12 months** before you start, or set a firm budget that slows your spending once you're there.
- **Make it a household decision.** If you have a partner, their income, job security, and tolerance for risk matter as much as yours. Two incomes, with one of them stable, change the picture completely.
- **Set exit criteria in advance.** For example: "If funding isn't secured by month 12, or my savings drop below 4 months, I start looking." Deciding this ahead of time keeps sunk-cost thinking from running the clock out.

## Bottom line

**Leaning yes:** the startup is well funded (18+ months of runway), the cash pay is close to what you make now, health coverage is solid, your household has another income or you can build more savings, and you're drawn to the work itself rather than just escaping your current job.

**Leaning no, or not yet:** the company is early or underfunded, the pay cut is large, you're the only earner, or the main draw is equity. In that case, take a few months to grow your savings, negotiate better terms, or wait for a later-stage opportunity.
