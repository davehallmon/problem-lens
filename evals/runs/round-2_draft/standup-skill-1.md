**Summary.** "Async or not" is a proposed fix. The likely root is that one 30-minute meeting serves 14 people across 3 time zones, which costs about 7 engineer-hours a day, and most of that time is spent on updates that don't concern most listeners. Assuming the engineers work in sub-teams and most of the meeting is status, not problem-solving.

**Recommendation.**
- Do this first: Row 1 — split into smaller standups by sub-team, because it cuts the time cost without removing live contact.
- Switch to Row 2 if one time zone joins outside its working hours, or if sub-teams depend on each other every day.

| Priority | Solution | Lens | Why This Lens | Rationale | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Split into 2–3 standups of 4–6 people by sub-team or time zone, 10–15 minutes each, with one short weekly cross-team sync. | TRIZ / TIPS | Saving time and keeping coordination pull against each other. | Smaller groups remove the updates people don't need and keep live talk where work overlaps. | Work that crosses sub-teams may slip through unless the weekly sync catches it. |
| 2 | Run async updates with one sub-team for 3 weeks, compare against agreed signals, then keep, adjust, or drop. | PDCA | The worry about coordination is a guess you can test small. | A short trial replaces debate with evidence and is easy to undo. | Three weeks may be too short to show slow harms like duplicated work. |
| 3 | Replace the round of status with a shared task board; the standup only covers blocked items and handoffs. | First Principles | The daily round of updates is treated as the only way to coordinate. | Coordination needs only blockers and dependencies, so status can move to the board. | If the board goes stale, the team loses status and the meeting both. |
| 4 | Before changing anything, define 2–3 coordination signals (time blocked, duplicate work, missed handoffs) and track them for two weeks. | Six Thinking Hats | The time-savings view dominates; the risk view has no measure yet. | You can't tell if a change hurt coordination without a baseline. | Tracking adds overhead and may measure the wrong things. |
| 5 | Log two weeks of standups: for each item, note whether it led to action by someone other than the speaker. | Root Cause Analysis | The cost is visible but what the meeting actually does is not. | The log shows which parts of the meeting are worth keeping live. | A small sample may miss rare but important saves. |
| Anchor | Cap the current standup at 15 minutes and move any discussion to a follow-up with only the people involved. | Occam's Razor | Half the time saved with no new process. | It cuts cost tomorrow with no tools, trials, or restructuring. | Fourteen updates in 15 minutes may become rushed and less useful. |

Ranked by: Coverage (default). Other options: Impact vs. Effort, Confidence vs. Reversibility, ICE, RICE, Eisenhower, Regret Minimization, Optionality, Cost of Delay.

**Questions That Would Change the Pick**
1. Do the engineers fall into sub-teams with few daily dependencies on each other? If not, Row 1 loses its benefit and Row 3 moves up.
2. Does any time zone join the standup outside its normal working hours? If yes, Row 2 (async trial) should come first.
3. Is most of the 30 minutes status, or live problem-solving? If mostly problem-solving, the meeting is doing real work and Row 5 should come first to find out what.
