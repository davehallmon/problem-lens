# Analysis: A Nine-Person Board That Can't Converge on a Fundraising Plan

## The core diagnosis

Your board doesn't lack fundraising ideas. It lacks a way to decide. When every meeting ends with a new idea, the board is brainstorming again each time instead of choosing. Nobody has said what a good plan has to accomplish, so no idea can win. Each new proposal gets judged on how exciting it sounds in the room, and the newest one usually sounds the most exciting.

Not having a development director makes this worse in two ways:

1. **No one owns the work between meetings.** Ideas come up, but nobody turns them into costed options, so the next meeting starts over.
2. **No one has the data to settle arguments.** Without someone tracking donor retention, average gift size, and return by channel, every opinion carries the same weight.

## What the numbers suggest

- **The gap.** If most of the $400k comes from donations, a 15% drop is roughly $50–60k a year. The real figure depends on how much of the budget is grants, earned revenue, or events, and the board should know it exactly. A $60k problem calls for a different plan than a $200k problem.
- **The cause of the drop matters more than the plan.** Did you lose a few major donors, or many small ones? Did fewer donors give, or did the same donors give less? Was one big event cancelled? Each answer points to a different fix:
  - **A few major donors lapsed:** personal outreach by board members. This is cheap and fast.
  - **Broad small-donor attrition:** better stewardship and communication, or a recurring-giving program.
  - **One revenue source collapsed:** replace or rebuild that source specifically.
- **Capacity is the binding constraint.** With no development staff, any plan that needs heavy staff execution, such as a gala, a big direct-mail campaign, or several grant applications, will be done by volunteers or by an executive director who is already stretched. Choose a plan sized to the people who will actually do it.

## Why boards of nine get stuck

- **Diffuse ownership.** With nine people, everyone can propose and no one has to commit.
- **Avoiding conflict.** Choosing one plan means telling someone their idea lost. Adding another idea feels kinder than ruling one out.
- **No decision rule.** Is it consensus, majority, or the chair's call? If nobody knows, discussion keeps going by default.
- **Mixed roles.** Some members expect to give, some to ask, some only to govern. A plan that assumes everyone will make asks will quietly fail.

## A process to get to one plan

**Step 1: Agree on the problem before the solution (one meeting, or an email beforehand).**
Have the treasurer or ED bring a one-page donor analysis: revenue by source over three years, donor count, retention rate, and the top 20 donors' giving trends. Get the board to agree on the target dollar amount and the main cause of the decline.

**Step 2: Set the criteria before reopening ideas.**
For example, a plan must:
- Close at least $X of the gap within 12 months
- Be executable with current people (name who)
- Cost no more than $Y up front
- Build something that lasts, such as donor relationships or recurring revenue, rather than a one-time event

**Step 3: Freeze the idea list.**
Collect every idea from past meetings, which is probably 5–10. Allow no new ideas after this point. Have two board members, or a small ad hoc committee, score each one against the criteria and bring back the top two or three, each with cost, expected return, and who would own it.

**Step 4: Decide with a known rule on a known date.**
Announce in advance: "At the [month] meeting we will vote, and the plan with a majority will be adopted." Ask anyone who disagrees to say so before or during that meeting, not afterward.

**Step 5: Assign an owner and a review date.**
One board member, or the ED, owns the plan. The board reviews progress quarterly against specific metrics. New ideas go into a "next year" parking lot rather than reopening the decision.

## Likely shape of a good plan (to test, not adopt blindly)

For an organization this size with no development staff, the evidence-backed default is usually:
- **Board give/get commitments.** Every member gives personally and makes a set number of asks or introductions. This is often the fastest and cheapest money available.
- **Major-donor retention.** Personal calls and visits to your top 20–30 donors, especially any who lapsed.
- **A recurring/monthly giving program**, which steadies revenue over time.
- **Think about a part-time or contract development person** if the numbers show a return. At $400k, even a modest hire can pay for itself if it brings back lapsed donors.

Be skeptical of a new signature event as the main answer. Events are labor-intensive and often net less than they gross.

## Questions the board should answer honestly

1. What does each board member personally commit to give and do?
2. Is the real disagreement about fundraising tactics, or about something underneath, like the organization's direction, the ED's role, or how much work the board should do?
3. Who has authority to call the question and end debate?
4. What happens if the board still can't agree by the deadline? (Pre-agree a fallback, such as the chair or executive committee deciding.)

## Bottom line

Stop debating plans. First agree on the size and cause of the gap, the criteria for a good plan, and how and when the decision will be made. Once those three things are settled, picking a plan becomes quick. Without them, the tenth idea will lose to the eleventh the same way the first nine did.
