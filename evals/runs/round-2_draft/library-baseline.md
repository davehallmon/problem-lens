# Getting Teens Back to the Library After School

## The real problem

The goal is stated as "more teenagers using the branch," but the most important fact in your background is the last one: **staff have never asked teens what they want.** Until that changes, any pilot is a guess. With only one pilot's worth of budget, spending it on a guess is the biggest risk you face.

So the problem has two parts:
1. **Diagnosis:** Why did teen visits fall by half since 2022?
2. **Intervention:** What single pilot is most likely to reverse that?

Most teams skip the first part. Don't.

## What might explain a 50% drop

A drop this large over a few years usually has more than one cause. Treat these as hypotheses to check, not conclusions:

- **Competing spaces:** a new rec center, coffee shop, after-school program, or other free hangout nearby.
- **Access changes:** bus routes, school bell times, branch hours, safety on the walk there, or new school-day schedules.
- **Post-pandemic habit loss:** teens who were 11–12 in 2020–2022 never formed a library habit, and the older cohort aged out.
- **Policy or atmosphere:** stricter noise or food rules, a security presence, ID requirements, or staff seen as unwelcoming to groups of teens.
- **Offer mismatch:** few things teens actually need (reliable Wi-Fi, outlets, group study space, printing, gaming, maker tools, quiet space, snacks).
- **Digital substitution:** homework and socializing moved fully online.

Some of these you can check cheaply from data you already have: hours, gate counts by time of day, program attendance, card registrations by age, and incident logs.

## Step 1: Ask teens first (low cost, high value)

Spend a small, fixed share of the pilot budget and 3–4 weeks on listening before choosing the intervention:

- **Talk to non-users, not just current users.** The teens still coming are the least informative group. Go to the schools, the rec center, the places teens now spend afternoons.
- **Use short, low-friction formats:** a 5-question survey through school librarians or counselors, a few informal conversations, a pizza-and-feedback session.
- **Pay or reward participation** (gift cards, food, volunteer hours). Teens' time is worth something.
- **Ask about their afternoons, not about the library.** "Where do you go after school, and why there?" reveals more than "What programs would you like?"
- **Recruit 4–6 teens as a standing advisory group.** This also builds ownership, and teens bring friends.

## Step 2: Pick one pilot based on what you hear

Options that commonly work, depending on what the listening reveals:

| If teens say... | A pilot that fits |
|---|---|
| "There's nowhere to hang out" | A dedicated teen space or after-school hours with relaxed noise and food rules |
| "I need help with school" | Homework help, tutoring, or test-prep sessions with peers or volunteers |
| "It's boring" | Gaming, maker, music, or creative programs, ideally co-designed with teens |
| "I can't get there" or "It's not open" | Changed hours, a transit partnership, or pop-ups at schools |
| "I don't feel welcome" | Staff training, a teen-friendly staff contact, and revised conduct rules |

A strong default if you can't wait: **a teen-designed after-school space with food,** because it addresses several causes at once (somewhere to go, a reason to come, a sense of welcome).

## Step 3: Design the pilot to teach you something

- **Define success up front:** for example, average weekday teen visits from 3–6 p.m., repeat visitors, and new card sign-ups among 13–18 year olds. Set a target (such as recovering 25% of lost visits) before launching.
- **Get a baseline now** so you can compare before and after.
- **Run it long enough:** at least one full semester. Teen habits form slowly, and the first few weeks will undercount.
- **Tell teens where they already are:** school announcements, teacher referrals, and teen-run social posts outperform library newsletters.
- **Close the loop:** show teens what changed because of their input. That alone earns return visits.

## Risks to watch

- **Building what adults think teens want.** The main failure mode for teen services.
- **Mistaking a small bump for success.** Novelty draws a crowd once; repeat visits are the real signal.
- **Staff friction.** If staff aren't on board with more noise and groups, the space will feel unwelcoming within weeks. Involve them early.
- **Scaling too soon.** One pilot that works is evidence for that branch and that group of teens, not proof for every branch.

## Bottom line

The highest-value move is cheap: **ask teens, especially the ones who stopped coming, before spending the pilot budget.** Then run one focused, teen-shaped pilot with clear metrics and a full semester to prove itself.
