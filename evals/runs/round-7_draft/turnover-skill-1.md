**Summary.** The split between "plan" and "try things" is a symptom; the root is that no one yet knows why nurses leave, since exit interviews give mixed reasons. Each camp is partly right: some causes are known and can be planned for, while others need small tests to find. Assuming the unit has exit records you can sort and some leadership time each week, the first move is to find where the exits cluster.

**What's Likely Going On**
- One or two local drivers, such as a shift pattern, staffing ratio, or a single manager, hide under the mixed exit reasons (guess). Check: sort exits by shift, manager, and unit area; a tight cluster confirms this, an even spread rules it out.
- Most exits are new nurses leaving in their first year, a common pattern in hospital nursing (guess). Check: the share of leavers with under 12 months of tenure.
- Outside pull, such as higher pay or travel contracts, draws nurses away no matter how the unit runs (guess). Check: where leavers went and the pay gap with nearby hospitals.

**Recommendation**
- Do this first: Row 1 — sort past exits by tenure, shift, manager, and stated reason, because it tells the three explanations apart at almost no cost.
- Switch to Row 2 if exit records are too thin or vague to sort.

| Priority | Solution | Lens | Why This Lens | Rationale | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sort the last 12 to 24 months of exits by tenure, shift, manager, and stated reason, and look for clusters. | Root Cause Analysis | Mixed exit reasons show that nurses leave but hide why. | A cluster points to a cause you can act on; no cluster points to outside pull. | Exit reasons may be polite rather than true, so clusters by tenure and manager matter more than stated reasons. |
| 2 · Anchor | Have charge nurses ask each current nurse two questions this month: what would make you leave, and what keeps you here. | Occam's Razor | The simplest source of the cause is the nurses who have not left yet. | It costs only a few minutes per nurse and reaches people while they can still be kept. | Nurses may not speak freely to someone in their chain, so a neutral asker may be needed. |
| 3 | In one leadership meeting, split the causes into known ones to plan for (pay, scheduling rules) and unclear ones to test (morale, support). | Cynefin, Six Thinking Hats | The two camps disagree on what kind of problem this is, and each method fits one part. | It ends the either-or debate by giving each camp the part its method suits. | It needs Row 1 or Row 2 results first, or the sorting is just opinion. |
| 4 | Run one 90-day pilot aimed at the top cluster, such as self-scheduling or a new-nurse mentor, and track intent to stay and first-year exits. | PDCA | The right fix is still a guess, and small tests can confirm it. | It lets the "try things" camp act while still producing data the "plan" camp can trust. | Assuming about 40 nurses, 28% turnover means roughly 3 exits a quarter, too few to judge a pilot by turnover alone, so it needs leading measures. |
| 5 | Give a small group of staff nurses a set budget and 90 days to choose and run one retention fix themselves. | Divergent Thinking | Both camps assume leadership picks the fix; this changes who acts. | Nurses know the daily pain points, and owning the fix can itself raise commitment. | Leadership must accept a fix it did not choose, or trust will drop further. |
| 6 | Work out what one nurse exit costs (hiring, orientation, agency cover) and set the retention budget from that number. | First Principles | The debate assumes the question is how to act, not how much keeping a nurse is worth. | A cost per exit lets the team size spending on fact rather than argue about it. | It needs finance data on agency and orientation costs that the unit may not hold. |

**Questions That Would Change the Pick**
1. Do your exit records show tenure, shift, and manager? If not, Row 2 moves to first.
2. How many nurses are on the unit? A much larger unit would make turnover itself usable to judge the Row 4 pilot; a smaller one makes leading measures essential.
3. Are most leavers in their first year? If yes, a new-nurse onboarding and mentor pilot becomes the clear Row 4 test and moves up.
