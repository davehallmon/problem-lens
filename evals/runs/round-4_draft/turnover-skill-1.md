**1. Summary**

The leadership split is a symptom. The root is that no one knows yet why nurses leave, so each side is guessing how much is known. Assuming you can pull HR data on leavers by tenure, shift, and manager, and assuming pay is near market, the first move is to find out who is leaving before choosing between "plan" and "try."

**2. What's Likely Going On**

- Turnover is concentrated in one group, most likely nurses in their first year (guess, common on hospital units) — check: the share of the past year's leavers with under 12 months' tenure.
- Workload drives it: overtime, short staffing, or floating, with exit interviews giving softer, "safe" reasons (guess) — check: whether overtime hours and missed staffing targets rose in the months before departures, and whether leavers took bedside jobs at other hospitals.
- One shift or one manager drives it — check: turnover rate split by shift and by direct manager; a cause on one shift or under one manager will show as a gap well above the unit average.

**3. Recommendation**

- Do this first: Row 1 — split last year's leavers by tenure, shift, and manager, because it tells you which of the three explanations is true within a week, and that settles much of the plan-vs-try debate.
- Switch to Row 6 if the data shows departures follow overtime spikes or most leavers moved to bedside jobs elsewhere.

**6. Questions That Would Change the Pick**

1. Did most leavers have under a year on the unit? If yes, Row 4's test should target new-nurse onboarding and preceptor support, and it would move to first after Row 1.
2. Where did leavers go: bedside jobs at other hospitals, or out of bedside nursing? Other hospitals points to pay or conditions and moves Row 6 up; leaving bedside points to burnout and keeps the tests first.
3. Is your pay below local market? If yes, a pay review comes before any test, since tests will not offset a pay gap.
